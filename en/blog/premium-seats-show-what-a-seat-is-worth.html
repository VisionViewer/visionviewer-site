Premium cinema seats: show what a seat is worth

Recliners, love seats, center rows, premium auditoriums: premium cinema seats cost more than the rest. On a seat map, though, they look like every other seat. What we know about moviegoers’ willingness to pay for a seat, what they are missing when they decide, and what a view from the seat can show.

In short

A premium seat is a seat sold at a higher price for its comfort or its position: a wider recliner, a seat for two, a row in the center of the house. The moviegoer pays for it before sitting in it. On a typical seat map, a recliner is one square like any other; its value does not show. A 3D view from the seat shows what you will see from that seat: the screen, how much of your field of view it fills, the rows in front. The price itself is still set by the cinema’s ticketing system.

What counts as a premium cinema seat?

The term covers several offers, which cinemas often combine:

  • The recliner, or club seat. Wider and more widely spaced than a standard seat, often reclining. A standard seat might be 56 cm (22 in) wide and a club seat 90 cm (35 in): it is no longer the same auditorium.
  • The love seat, or double seat. A seat for two or a small bench, with no armrest between the two moviegoers. It sells as one unit, for two.
  • The center row. A standard seat, but where picture and sound are best, sold with an upcharge: this is seat-based pricing, by location.
  • The premium auditorium. The whole room is above average: large screen, immersive sound, recliners. The upcharge applies to the screening, but the seat matters there as much as anywhere.

For a cinema, the recliner seats upsell is the clearest case: the gap between a standard seat and a recliner is large, and so is the price difference.

Why do moviegoers hesitate to pay more?

Because they pay for a difference they cannot see. When choosing, they look at a seat map: squares, row letters, sometimes a color or an icon for premium seats. They have to guess the rest.

  • The map does not show proportions. On most seat maps, ours included, a seat takes one square whatever its width. A recliner and a standard seat are the same size there.
  • A row letter does not give the distance. Row G is mid-house in one auditorium and at the back of another. “Center row” says neither how far you will be from the screen nor at what angle you will see it.
  • The upcharge is certain; the benefit is not. Paying more for a seat you have never seen is a bet on a description.

This reasoning has not been measured for cinemas: we know of no study on the effect of a view from the seat on premium seat sales. The figures below only show that moviegoers put a value on where they sit.

What the numbers say about a seat’s value

Surveyed by Morning Consult in March 2023, 48% of U.S. adults say they prefer seats in the middle; a quarter prefer the back third, and 8% the front third.

The same year, the U.S. chain AMC tested different prices depending on the seat. More than three in four members of its loyalty program who already bought center seats kept doing so, despite the upcharge. The price cut on the front row attracted almost no additional moviegoers. AMC ended the test.

Both results point the same way: moviegoers who care about their seat know which one they want, and a discount does not make an unwanted seat desirable. They do not say how many moviegoers would move to a pricier seat if they could see the difference.

What objectively makes a good seat?

Auditorium design standards give numeric benchmarks, covered in detail in our article on the best seat in a movie theater:

  • Screen angle. The picture looks small below about 30° of horizontal field of view, according to SMPTE guideline EG 18-1994; THX recommends 36° from the farthest seat.
  • Looking up. Beyond 35° to see the top of the picture, most moviegoers are uncomfortable, according to the same guideline.
  • Sideways offset. The design guide published by UNIC, the international union of cinemas, rates a sideways angle to the screen center below 35° as good, and up to 45° as acceptable.

These benchmarks play out differently in every auditorium. In the auditorium of our demo, with a screen 9.40 m (31 ft) wide:

What a 3D view from the seat shows

VisionViewer computes, in the browser, the view from every seat, from the auditorium’s real dimensions. The camera sits at the eyes of a seated moviegoer, 1.15 m (3.8 ft) above the floor of their row, looking at the center of the screen. For a premium seat, that shows:

  • Distance and angle. The screen appears at the size it will have from that seat: you see whether it fills your view or whether you will be looking up.
  • The rows in front. The rake of the stepped floor and the row spacing are those of the auditorium.
  • Seat width. Seat width and the gap between seats are part of the auditorium’s dimensions: a room of recliners does not look like a standard room.
  • Double seats. A love seat has a single identifier and counts as two. In 3D it appears as a bench, and the view is taken from between the two moviegoers.
  • Comparison. The arrows move the chosen group of seats without changing its shape: you step from a premium row to a standard one and see what the price difference buys.

The view opens before payment, at the moment the moviegoer hesitates. It adds to the cinema’s own seat map (VisionViewer.open()), or comes with our 2D seat map for a cinema that has none (VisionViewer.mountPlan()).

Who sets the price of premium seats?

The cinema’s ticketing system, and only it. VisionViewer handles no prices, no showtimes and no sales: we store only the shape of the auditoriums. The cinema decides which seats are premium and at what price; its ticketing system displays it and decides at payment. The 3D view shows the seat, not its price.

To measure, the cinema has our daily statistics per auditorium: seat maps shown, 3D views opened, choices confirmed and, if it reports them, orders. They say nothing about the moviegoer, nor about the price paid.

What this article does not say

  • No measured gain. We know of no study on the effect of a 3D view on premium seat sales, and we have no customer results to publish.
  • The poll and the price test are American. AMC’s test covered members of its loyalty program.
  • The model has limits. An auditorium is described with one seat width and one row spacing; a room mixing a row of recliners with standard rows cannot yet be described row by row. Straight rows, a constant rake, a single level: no balcony, no curved rows.
  • The view does not show how far a seat reclines or how comfortable it is: it shows what you see from it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a recliner or club seat in a cinema?

A seat wider and more widely spaced than a standard one, often reclining, sold at a higher price. For example 90 cm (35 in) wide, against 56 cm (22 in) for a standard seat.

Are premium cinema seats worth the price?

It depends on the auditorium. A center seat about two-thirds of the way back sees the screen at a comfortable angle without looking up; a recliner in the front row can force you to look up more than 35°, the discomfort threshold in SMPTE guideline EG 18-1994. The best check is to see the screen from the seat before paying.

Will moviegoers pay more for a better seat?

Some will. In a 2023 price test by AMC, more than three in four loyalty members who already bought center seats kept buying them despite the upcharge. 48% of U.S. adults prefer seats in the middle, according to Morning Consult.

Does VisionViewer set seat prices?

No. VisionViewer shows the 3D view from each seat; the cinema’s ticketing system sets prices, displays them and validates the sale at payment.
