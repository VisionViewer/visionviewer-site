What is the best seat in a movie theater?

Center, about two-thirds of the way back: the advice is well known. It has a precise origin, in the standards used to build and tune auditoriums. Here is what they say, in degrees and in meters, and why the right row is not the same from one auditorium to the next.

In short

The best seat in a movie theater is in the center of the row, between the middle and two-thirds of the way back. That is where the sound is tuned, and where the screen fills a large part of your field of view without making you tilt your head back.

Sound: why “two-thirds back”

The two-thirds advice comes from sound tuning. To tune an auditorium, a technician places the primary microphone near the screen’s centerline, about two-thirds of the distance from the screen to the back wall. That is what recommended practice RP 2096-1 asks for, from SMPTE, the body that standardizes cinema. The loudspeakers behind the screen are aimed toward that point too.

Dolby makes it the “reference listening position” in the Dolby Atmos specifications: a point two-thirds of the distance to the rear wall, generally on the screen’s centerline. It is at that point that Dolby sets the sound level each screen loudspeaker must be able to reach.

One caveat: those same documents ask for measurements in several places, because the goal is even sound across a large part of the auditorium. Two-thirds back is the tuning point, not the only good seat.

Picture: at what angle do you see the screen?

For the picture, what matters is the angle the screen takes up in your field of view: the angle between its left and right edges, seen from the seat. The farther back you sit, the narrower it gets.

  • In the last row, at least 30°. SMPTE’s guideline EG 18-1994 considers that the image looks small if it fills less than about 30° of the horizontal field of view. It describes auditoriums where it fills 30° for the farthest viewer, and up to 80° for the nearest ones.
  • 36° for THX. THX recommends that the screen be seen at a 36-degree angle from the farthest seat in the auditorium.

Front or back?

Too close, your neck decides. According to the SMPTE guideline, most viewers are uncomfortable when they have to look up more than 35° to see the top of the image. In France, the CST, which awards the Excellence and Immersion labels, denies its label to an auditorium where the first row is closer than half the widest image width (“0.5 × Lmax” in its grid), or from which viewers have to tilt their head more than 45° toward the top of the screen.

Too far, the image shrinks. At the back of a deep auditorium, the screen drops below the SMPTE guideline’s 30°: you see everything, but you are no longer in it.

On the sides, the image is distorted. The design guide distributed by UNIC, the International Union of Cinemas, rates a lateral angle to the center of the screen as good below 35°, acceptable up to 45°, and unacceptable beyond.

Between those limits, the French academic Claude Forest sums up the constraints of picture and sound this way: in an auditorium built to the standards, the best seats are in the center across the width, and between one-third and two-thirds of the way back.

Where to sit in an IMAX or giant-screen theater

Neither IMAX nor Dolby Cinema publishes, on its website, a numerical rule about which seat to choose: we found none.

What is known comes from the Giant Screen Cinema Association (GSCA). According to its specifications, the last row of such a theater must be within one screen width of the screen, which gives a viewing angle of at least 53°; it says this is based on IMAX’s original theater designs. In other words, the back of a giant-screen theater sees the screen at a much wider angle than the back of a conventional auditorium.

The consequence is simple: in a giant-screen theater, you can sit farther back without losing the image. The center of the middle and back rows spares you from tilting your head.

What moviegoers actually choose

Surveyed in March 2023, 48% of U.S. adults say they prefer seats in the middle; a quarter prefer the back third, and 8% the front third (Morning Consult poll, 2,203 adults).

The same year, the U.S. chain AMC tested different prices depending on the seat. More than three in four members of its loyalty program who already bought the center seats kept doing so, despite the upcharge. The price cut on the front row attracted almost no additional moviegoers. AMC ended the test.

In France, Claude Forest observed 108 screenings and 3,501 moviegoers in three cinemas, in 2010. The first to arrive, who have every choice, do not head for the technically best seats. In a 66-seat auditorium, the five most often chosen seats were all in the last two rows, and the front row was never chosen by the first arrivals.

Why the right row changes from one auditorium to the next

A row letter says nothing on its own. Row G is the middle of one auditorium and the back of another; it all depends on the width of the screen, the depth of the room and the slope of the floor.

How to pick your seat, in practice

  1. Aim for the center of the row. The image is not distorted there, and the sound is at its most balanced.
  2. In depth, aim between the middle and two-thirds of the way back.
  3. Avoid the very first rows if you do not like tilting your head back.
  4. In a giant-screen theater, sit farther back.
  5. If the cinema’s website shows the view from the seat, look at it before you pay.

Frequently asked questions

Which row is best in a movie theater?

There is no universal row letter: the right row depends on the auditorium. Aim for the center, between the middle and two-thirds of the way back. Two-thirds back is where the sound is tuned, according to SMPTE and Dolby documents, and the screen still fills a large part of your field of view there.

Is it better to sit in the front or the back of a movie theater?

Neither. In the front, you have to tilt your head back: discomfort starts beyond 35° toward the top of the image, according to SMPTE guideline EG 18-1994. At the back of a deep auditorium, the screen fills less than 30° of your field of view and looks small.

Where should you sit in an IMAX theater?

IMAX does not publish a numerical rule. According to the specifications of the Giant Screen Cinema Association (GSCA), the last row of such a theater is within one screen width of the screen: even at the back, the image stays very wide. The center of the middle and back rows spares you from tilting your head.

How far from the screen should you sit in a movie theater?

Between 0.6 and 1.9 times the width of the screen. At 0.6 screen widths, the screen fills 80° of your field of view, the limit of comfort in the first row according to the UNIC guide. At 1.9 screen widths, it fills 30°, the minimum of the SMPTE guideline. THX recommends 36° in the last row, or 1.5 screen widths.

Where is the sound best in a movie theater?

About two-thirds of the way back, on the screen’s centerline. That is the point where the sound is measured and tuned, according to SMPTE recommended practice RP 2096-1 and the Dolby Atmos specifications.

What these sources do not say

  • SMPTE guideline EG 18-1994 was withdrawn in 2003. It remains available as an archive, but SMPTE no longer endorses it. Its angles are still used as landmarks.
  • The French standard NF S 27-001, on which the CNC’s approval of auditoriums relies, is paid: we have not read it. We cite the criteria of the CST labels, which are public.
  • The “THX” angles of 26° or 40° that are often quoted: we did not find them in a THX source. Its page only gives 36°.
  • IMAX and Dolby Cinema do not publish the geometry of their auditoriums.
  • The poll and the pricing test are American. The French study is an observation from 2010, in three cinemas.
