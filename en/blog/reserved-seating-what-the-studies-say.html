The seat matters before the purchase: what the studies say

Choosing a seat has become a reason to buy a movie ticket online, and to pick one cinema over another. Here is what two published studies let us state, and what they do not say.

Online buyers choose their seat first

The largest study on the subject was commissioned by Webedia Movies Pro and carried out by Vertigo Research: more than 100,000 moviegoers surveyed as they left US cinemas, throughout 2018. It compares those who bought their ticket online with those who bought it on site.

Among the former, 28% say they chose that cinema because it lets them reserve their seat. Among those who bought at the box office, only 7.6% do. The gap is almost one to four: for the moviegoer who books from a phone, the seat is not a detail of the order, it is one of the reasons to place it there rather than elsewhere.

Six years later, Fandango’s annual study points the same way. Conducted in February 2024 among more than 6,000 US moviegoers, it ranks reserved seating first among the reasons to buy online or in an app, ahead of skipping the line.

Moviegoers who find their showtime on your site buy there

The same 2018 study follows the moviegoer from search to purchase. When they find their showtime on the cinema’s website, they complete their purchase on that same site 72% of the time.

In other words, the cinema’s own booking flow is where the sale is decided. Anything that takes the moviegoer out of it, a comparison site, one more tab, a hesitation about the seat, is a chance to lose them.

Online buying grows, the box office recedes

Still according to that study, online ticket purchases grew by 18.7% in 2018, while box-office purchases fell by 5.4%. The shift predates 2020; it has not reversed since.

For a growing share of the audience, the seat map has therefore become the first contact with the auditorium itself.

What these figures do not say

  • They come from the United States. Booking habits differ from one country to another. For France, the CNC publishes other measurements, which we present in a separate piece.
  • They date from 2018 and 2024. They give a direction, not a measurement of today.
  • They are statements. A moviegoer who says they choose a cinema for reserved seating does not necessarily do so every time.
  • They do not measure VisionViewer. None of these studies is about a 3D view from the seat. They establish that the seat matters; they do not say what seeing it changes.
